# How Words Are Mapped: An Explanation of the OCR Text Alignment Pipeline

> **Scope:** This document describes **alignment logic inside this repository** only. For where CUDA/GPU applies in a full PDF→VLM→ALTO workflow, and how to run without NVIDIA hardware, see **[FULL_PIPELINE_AND_CUDA.md](FULL_PIPELINE_AND_CUDA.md)**.

When working with historical documents, we face a fundamental challenge: the text extracted by optical character recognition (OCR) is often imperfect, containing errors, split words, and formatting quirks. Meanwhile, we have a clean, corrected version of the text—perhaps produced by a language model that has carefully reviewed and corrected the content. The task is to map each word from the clean text back to its corresponding location in the original OCR output, creating a bridge between the corrected version and the document's physical structure.

The mapping process begins with what we might call "first impressions." Each word detected by OCR is compared against the entire vocabulary of clean text words, looking for close matches. This initial fuzzy matching finds words that are similar—perhaps "unconscous" in the OCR text matches "unconscious" in the clean text, or "resi-" (a word broken at a line break) might match "residence." But this first pass is just the beginning, because many words will have multiple possible matches, and some words won't match at all.

The real intelligence of the system comes from understanding context. Words don't exist in isolation; they have neighbors. The system examines triplets—a word and its immediate neighbors on both sides. If an OCR word "X" sits between words "A" and "B" in the document, and a clean text word "Y" sits between words "A" and "B" in the corrected version, that's strong evidence that X and Y are the same word, even if they look quite different. This context-based matching can resolve ambiguities that fuzzy matching alone cannot.

But historical documents are messy. Words get split across lines with hyphens—sometimes visible hyphens like "uncon-scious," sometimes invisible splits where "dams" (mistakenly recognized by the OCR from 'dam-' due to bleed through of text from the other side of the page) on one line and "aged" on the next should actually be read as "damaged. The system must recognize these patterns and link the fragments back together. Similarly, OCR sometimes runs words together, creating a single word where there should be two, or splits a word incorrectly. The system searches for these patterns, testing whether combining or splitting words would create better matches.

Here's where the process becomes cyclical, and this is crucial to understanding how it works. As words get matched, their neighbors become easier to match. When we successfully link "dams" and "aged" into "damaged," we now know what words surround "damaged" in both the OCR and clean texts. Those neighbors can then be matched more confidently, which in turn helps match their neighbors, and so on. The system runs through multiple iterations, each time using the newly resolved words to help resolve others, until the mapping stabilizes.

For words that still resist matching—perhaps because they're so corrupted by OCR errors that even context can't help—the system employs a clever fallback strategy. It looks at the physical dimensions of the word on the page. If an unmatched clean text word should be about ten characters wide based on the average character size in that paragraph, and there's an unmatched OCR word that's approximately that width, and the neighbors match up strongly, the system can make an educated guess. This "fill in the blank" approach uses the document's typography as evidence.

Throughout this process, the system must handle edge cases: words that appear multiple times in the clean text, paragraphs that appear in different orders, words at the edges of columns or pages. When multiple perfect matches exist for a word, the system considers sequence—does this match maintain the proper reading order?—and geometry—does this word appear in the expected physical position relative to its neighbors?

The result is a careful, iterative alignment that respects both the linguistic structure of the text and the physical layout of the document. Each word in the clean text is mapped to its corresponding location in the OCR output, creating a precise correspondence that preserves the document's original structure while enabling access to the corrected content.
